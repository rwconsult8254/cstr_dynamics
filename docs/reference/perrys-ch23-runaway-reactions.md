# Perry's Chapter 23 (Process Safety): runaway reactions

*RAG-grounded summary from the process-engineering RAG, document Perrys_Ch23_Process_Safety, drafted 2026-09-23 for Roger's review. Own words; short quotations only.*

## Scope of this summary

What Chapter 23 says about runaway exothermic reactions: the definition, the
heat-generation versus heat-removal picture (Figs. 23-15 and 23-16), the
temperature of no return, the catalogue of upsets that start a runaway, the
role of the maximum adiabatic temperature rise in hazard assessment, and the
kinetic and thermal data a design needs. Relief sizing, calorimeter hardware,
storage of self-reactive substances, and endothermic processes are noted only
where they bear on the model. Stability theory proper is Chapter 19 and
reactor temperature control is Chapter 8; those are covered in sibling
summaries.

## What Perry's says

**Definition.** Perry's restricts the term runaway reaction to loss of control
of a kinetically limited exothermic reaction that normally runs at a stable,
controlled rate with adequate heat removal. When heat removal falls short,
the excess heat raises the temperature of the reaction mass, the rate and
therefore the heat release rise exponentially with temperature, and the
process bootstraps itself. Perry's gives three things that can halt it:
exhaustion of the limiting reactant, boil-off of a solvent, or quenching and
inhibition. The consequences named are temperature rise, gas generation,
vapour boil-off, exceedance of pressure or thermal limits, and initiation of
secondary reactions. [gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00147]

**The heat-balance picture.** Fig. 23-15 states the stable case: all heat
generated by the reaction is transferred to the surroundings. Fig. 23-16 adds
the upset cases for a reactor with a jacket or coil. Two shifts are drawn: a
rise in the coolant temperature, and a fall in the heat-transfer coefficient
such as by fouling. Either moves the heat-removal line. Once the shift passes
the temperature of no return, the removal line no longer crosses the
generation line and "stable operation is no longer possible". The figure
caption puts it as the generation rate, which rises exponentially with
temperature, exceeding the system's capacity to remove heat.
[gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00148, gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00154]

**Initiating upsets.** Perry's lists many abnormal situations that can start
a runaway. Those relevant to a jacketed CSTR are: loss of coolant flow; a
rise in coolant temperature; a general rise in the process temperature;
abnormal heat addition, including steam into the jacket; gradual fouling of
the heat-transfer surface until maximum coolant flow no longer suffices; loss
of agitation or any other reduction in the heat-transfer coefficient;
contaminant or excess catalyst raising the reaction rate; and excess or too
rapid addition of the limiting reactant. The chapter stresses that runaway
has no single mechanism and can occur in storage and purification equipment
as well as reactors. [gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00148,
gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00149, gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00150]

**Historical causes.** A UK analysis (Barton and Nolan) attributed thermal
runaway incidents to four general causes: inadequate understanding of the
chemistry and thermochemistry, inadequate design for heat removal, inadequate
control and safety systems, and inadequate procedures and training.
[gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00150]

**Design considerations for intended reactions.** Perry's, citing Hendershot
(2002), lists what a designer should know: the heat of reaction for the
intended and other possible reactions; the maximum adiabatic reaction
temperature; the stability of the mixture at that temperature; the heat
addition and removal capabilities of the reactor, including the agitator as a
heat source; the effect of deviations in reactant charge and operating
conditions; the maximum temperature of every connected heat source; the
minimum temperature the cooling sources can reach; and the rates of all
reactions. It also advises against using temperature control of the reaction
mixture as the means of limiting reaction rate.
[gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00144, gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00145,
gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00146]

**Worst-case thinking and the adiabatic rise.** The designer is told to
conceive the worst realistic combination of circumstances at every point of
operation, with loss of cooling water, loss of agitation, and instrument
failure among the examples, and to evaluate consequences so that the plant is
safe even if the worst case occurs. Calculating the maximum adiabatic
temperature rise and then asking what happens if it is realised is offered as
the model of this analysis. [gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00172,
gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00173]

**Data a model needs.** Reactivity testing aims at three quantities: the
temperature at which the exotherm starts, the rate of reaction as a function
of temperature, and the heat generated per unit mass. Onset temperature has
two meanings, one from the calorimeter and one for the reactor, where a
runaway is possible whenever net heat gain exceeds total heat-loss capability.
Accelerating rate calorimetry gives the self-heating rate under near-adiabatic
conditions and can usually yield activation energy, heat of reaction, and
approximate reaction order; adiabatic calorimeters can directly simulate
upsets such as loss of cooling. The relief section adds that most reactions
are Arrhenius in behaviour and that runaway can follow loss of cooling, feed
or quench failure, excessive feed rate or temperature, contaminants, or
agitation failure. [gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00173,
gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00174, gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00175,
gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00426, gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00415]

**Mitigation, for context only.** Semibatch operation with gradual addition
of the limiting reactant is presented as inherently safer, and containment is
rarely rated to hold a runaway, so relief is the last line of defence.
Neither applies to a constant-volume continuous reactor except as framing.
[gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00150, gpref-perrys-ch23-process-safety-00151]

## What it means for cstr_dynamics

Agent interpretation, not Perry's.

- **Upsets tab.** The Perry's list maps onto five upsets that the constant-
  volume model can express directly: loss of coolant flow (flow to zero or a
  stuck valve); coolant supply temperature rise; fouling (a step or ramp
  reduction in UA); loss of agitation (a larger step reduction in UA); and a
  feed disturbance (feed concentration or feed temperature step, the CSTR
  analogue of excess reactant addition). Steam into the jacket is a sixth if
  the jacket inlet temperature is an input.
- **What a runaway must look like.** After an upset past the temperature of no
  return, reactor temperature should rise at an accelerating rate with no
  new steady state reached until reactant depletion limits it. Feed of fresh
  reactant means depletion in a CSTR is only partial, so the trajectory
  should settle on the high-conversion, high-temperature branch rather than
  climb without bound. The behaviour list should say which.
- **Behaviour list and test spec.** Reproduce Fig. 23-16 numerically: plot
  heat generation and heat removal against reactor temperature for the design
  point, then for a raised coolant temperature and a reduced UA, and show the
  loss of the intersection. A test can assert the number of intersections
  before and after the upset. A second test can assert that the same upset
  applied below the threshold recovers and above it does not.
- **Parameters the safety framing constrains.** Heat of reaction and feed
  concentration set the adiabatic temperature rise, which Perry's makes the
  central hazard number; choose it so the runaway ceiling is dramatic but
  physically plausible for a liquid. Activation energy sets how steeply
  generation climbs and therefore how narrow the margin to the temperature of
  no return is. UA and nominal coolant flow set the cooling margin, and the
  design should leave the largest expected disturbance inside it.
- **Detection.** Perry's design guidance implies a high-temperature alarm;
  the dashboard can show one, but the brief does not ask for trips or relief,
  and they stay out of scope.
